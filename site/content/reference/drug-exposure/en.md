Drug exposure is a measure of how much drug is present in the body over time, usually summarized from concentrations as AUC, Cmax, Ctrough or an average concentration. The dose is the amount given; the exposure is what results, and it differs between patients who receive the same dose.

## Definition

- **Dose:** the amount administered, for example 200 mg or 2 mg/kg.
- **Exposure:** the concentration–time profile that follows, and the metrics that summarize it: [AUC](auc-area-under-the-curve.html) for cumulative exposure, [Cmax](cmax-peak-concentration.html) for the peak, [Ctrough](ctrough-trough-concentration.html) for the lowest concentration within an interval, and the average concentration.

Exposure is usually measured in plasma or serum. The concentration at the site of action, and the unbound fraction, may differ from it; see [unbound drug concentration](unbound-drug-concentration.html).

## Why it differs between patients

With the same dose, exposure depends on clearance and volume of distribution, and so on body weight, organ function, age, genetics, disease and co-medications ([drug interactions](drug-drug-interactions.html)). For an oral drug, absorption adds variability. This is why population PK models relate exposure, not only dose, to covariates.

## How it is used

- **Exposure–response analysis** relates exposure to efficacy and safety outcomes, to choose a dose or to justify an adjustment ([exposure–response](exposure-response.html)).
- **Metric choice:** the metric should match the biology, for example a peak for a peak-related event and a trough for a sustained target effect ([choosing an exposure metric](exposure-metric-selection.html)).
- **Safety margins:** exposure in animals at the no-effect dose is compared with expected human exposure ([exposure margins](exposure-margin.html)).

## Common pitfalls

- Equating a higher dose with higher exposure in every patient; clearance differences can make a lower dose in one patient match a higher dose in another.
- Comparing exposure metrics from different time windows, such as the first dose and steady state, as if they were the same quantity.
- Reading an exposure–outcome association as causal when clearance and disease severity are linked ([confounding](exposure-response-confounding.html)).
