Reference date: the facts in this article are as of 5 November 2026.

The objective response rate (the share of patients whose tumors shrink) is widely used as an indicator of effect in early trials, but it does not always move in the same direction as progression-free survival (PFS). Two randomized trials reported in 2026 are examples of the two directions. OptiTROP-Lung06, a phase 3 trial in first-line PD-L1-negative nonsquamous lung cancer (PD-L1 is an immune checkpoint molecule on tumor cells) comparing sacituzumab tirumotecan plus pembrolizumab with chemotherapy plus pembrolizumab, had a small difference in response rate (50.2% versus 47.3%) and a median PFS of 15.0 versus 5.7 months (hazard ratio 0.49) ([1](#ref-1)). By contrast, MIROVA (AGO-OVAR 2.34), a randomized phase 2 trial comparing carboplatin plus mirvetuximab soravtansine with carboplatin-containing chemotherapy in FRα-high (folate receptor alpha), platinum-eligible recurrent ovarian cancer, had about double the response rate (66.2% versus 32.8%) but no difference shown in median PFS (9.53 versus 9.79 months, hazard ratio 1.0) ([2](#ref-2)). This note recomputes the published numbers of the two trials by proportions and intervals, denominators and length of follow-up, to organize how to read a response rate. The two trials were chosen as cases in which the response rate and PFS did not match; the note does not show how often this happens, nor whether the response rate is valid as a surrogate.

## Scope and sources

For OptiTROP-Lung06 I read the abstract of the New England Journal of Medicine paper (Europe PMC record) and coverage of the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) 2026 presentation (Targeted Oncology); the full text could not be checked because the publisher's page refused the connection ([1](#ref-1), [3](#ref-3)). The trial was funded by Sichuan Kelun-Biotech (as stated in the abstract record). For MIROVA I read the full text of an OncLive report on the presentation at the ESMO Gynaecological Cancers Congress 2026 (June 2026). The result was also reported at the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) 2026 (J Clin Oncol 2026;44 suppl 16:5506), but I did not read that abstract. The funder and conflicts of interest of MIROVA are not given in the report and could not be confirmed ([2](#ref-2)). The values in the coverage have not been checked against the papers or presentations. Individual patient data are not public. The numbers are of two kinds. Values reported by the papers and coverage are quoted as printed. Values I calculated (estimated numbers of responders, intervals for differences, back-calculated hazard ratios) are marked as my calculation. The intervals are Newcombe 95% intervals for a difference of two proportions. Table 1 shows the published numbers of the two trials and their sources.

## Discussion by issue

### A small difference in response rate, but different shares of deep responses and durations of response

In OptiTROP-Lung06 the numbers of responders can be estimated as about 115 of 229 and 107 of 226 (estimates from the percentages, which may differ from the actual numbers), and the difference is 2.9 points (95% interval −6.3 to +12.0), an interval that includes 0 (my calculation). This interval does not show that there is no difference: it spans from a small disadvantage to an advantage of about 12 points. The share of deep responses (large tumor shrinkage) is 28.8% versus 17.3%, a difference of 11.6 points (3.8 to 19.1), and the median duration of response is 13.6 versus 6.8 months (hazard ratio 0.47) ([3](#ref-3)). The definition of deep response is not given in the coverage I read. The difference in response rate is small, but the share of deep responses and the duration of response differ, and a single value of the response rate does not contain the quality of response.

The PFS hazard ratio of 0.49 is consistent with other indicators. Back-calculating the hazard ratio from the 12-month PFS rates (51.1% versus 22.8%) under proportional hazards gives 0.45 (my calculation). The ratio of median PFS (15.0 ÷ 5.7 = 2.6) would correspond to a hazard ratio of 0.38 under an exponential assumption, but this is a different quantity; follow-up is short at 9.7 months, and the interval of the median in the sacituzumab tirumotecan arm runs from 9.9 months to not estimable ([3](#ref-3)). Whether the PFS difference comes from the depth and duration of response, from the time to progression of patients who did not respond, or from discontinuation cannot be separated from the published numbers; these are hypotheses.

![Figure 1. Response rates in the two trials: experimental arm (blue) and control arm (orange). A small difference in OptiTROP-Lung06 and about double in MIROVA (published numbers, my figure).](shared/orr-pfs-two-directions/figures/01-response-rates.png)

### A doubled response rate with no PFS difference shown

In MIROVA the response rate among evaluable patients is 66.2% (47 of 71) versus 32.8% (20 of 61), a difference of 33.4 points (16.4 to 47.8) and a ratio of 2.0 (my calculation). The median PFS is 9.53 versus 9.79 months with a hazard ratio of 1.0 (0.68 to 1.46). This interval excludes a risk reduction of more than 32% but includes an increase of up to 46%. In a small phase 2 trial, that no difference was shown does not show that there is no effect.

The denominator also needs attention. The response rate is for evaluable patients (71 and 61), but the PFS analysis includes 75 and 70 patients, with 4 and 9 not evaluable (75 and 70 are the PFS analysis sets, not necessarily all randomized patients). Counting non-evaluable patients as non-responders and using the PFS analysis population as the denominator gives 62.7% versus 28.6%, a difference of 34.1 points (18.0 to 47.8). Even counting all 9 non-evaluable control patients as responders (the view most favourable to the control arm), 41.4% versus 62.7% leaves a difference of 21.2 points (5.0 to 36.0) (my calculation). Whatever the handling of the denominator, the difference in response rate remains at odds with the absence of a PFS difference.

Other factors may also bear on the PFS result. The median follow-up is short, 9.2 and 8.9 months. For maintenance, 79.7% of the mirvetuximab arm received mirvetuximab and 42.9% of the control arm received a PARP inhibitor (an inhibitor of a DNA repair enzyme). Discontinuation for reasons other than progression was 10.8% versus 30.2%, but the report does not state how censoring was handled at discontinuation ([2](#ref-2)). Time to first subsequent therapy (TFST) had a hazard ratio of 0.64 (0.41 to 0.98), which did not agree with the 1.0 of PFS (exploratory and based on an open-label assessment). For OS, the curves crossed and restricted mean survival was used; the difference is 2.15 months (−3.10 to +7.40), with a maturity of 48.3%, and 35.7% of the control arm crossed over to mirvetuximab (crossover bears on OS). The report does not identify a reason for the PFS result.

### What the pairing of the two trials shows and does not show

The ratio of response rates is 1.06 in OptiTROP-Lung06 and 2.0 in MIROVA. The PFS hazard ratios are 0.49 and 1.0, so the size of the difference in response rate and the size of the difference in PFS move in opposite ways (Figures 1 and 2). The two trials, however, were chosen as cases in which response rate and PFS did not match, and they differ in disease, drug, control, size (455 and 145 patients) and follow-up. The pairing of two points shows that cases exist in which they do not match; it does not show how often this happens, nor whether the response rate is valid as a surrogate. The response rate is a proportion, whereas PFS is a time measure that includes depth and persistence of response, progression of patients who did not respond, and discontinuation. The two trials can be read as examples in which this difference can appear.

![Figure 2. PFS hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals for the same two trials (log axis). Below 1 favours the experimental arm (published numbers, my figure).](shared/orr-pfs-two-directions/figures/02-pfs-hazard-ratios.png)

### What is not reported

For OptiTROP-Lung06, intervals for the response rate, the definition of deep response, median OS and dosing were not reported in what I read. OS was immature at the first interim analysis, and the hazard ratio of 0.58 (0.38 to 0.88) is descriptive. For MIROVA, an interval for the response rate and the funder are not given in the report.

## Overall assessment

What could be confirmed as fact is as follows. In OptiTROP-Lung06 the difference in response rate is 2.9 points (interval −6.3 to +12.0), but the share of deep responses and the duration of response differ, and the PFS hazard ratio is 0.49. In MIROVA the response rate among evaluable patients is about double (66.2% versus 32.8%), and the PFS hazard ratio is 1.0 (0.68 to 1.46). A difference in response rate remains when the denominator is changed from evaluable patients to the PFS analysis population and further to the view most favourable to the control arm.

By interpretation: a single value of the response rate does not contain the quality and persistence of effect, so it does not necessarily move with PFS. The two trials are examples in which a mismatch can appear, and the short follow-up (around 9 months) limits how stable the results can be read. This article must not be used as a basis for an individual patient's treatment choice.

## Limitations

- The numbers are from abstracts and coverage. The full texts and the conference abstract could not be checked, and the coverage values have not been checked.
- The numbers of responders are estimates from the published percentages and may differ from the actual numbers. Hazard ratios back-calculated assume proportional hazards and depend on rounding.
- The two trials were chosen as cases in which response rate and PFS did not match, and they differ in disease, drug and design. The pairing is not a validation of the response rate as a surrogate and shows no frequency.
- MIROVA is a small phase 2 trial with short follow-up, and the handling of non-evaluable patients affects the denominator. Its funder and conflicts of interest could not be confirmed.
- This note makes no treatment recommendation. Toxicity and OS are outside its subject.
- The note was checked by three reviews performed by AI used by the author; this is not confirmation by a human third party.

## Conclusion

OptiTROP-Lung06 and MIROVA are cases in which the difference in response rate and the difference in PFS do not match, in size or in direction. In the former the difference in response rate is small and the PFS hazard ratio is 0.49; in the latter the response rate is about double and no PFS difference was shown. The response rate is best read together with its denominator, depth, persistence and follow-up, and not substituted for PFS.
