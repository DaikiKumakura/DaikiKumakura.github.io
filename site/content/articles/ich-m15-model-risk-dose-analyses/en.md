## Abstract {#article-abstract}

The guideline M15 of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) on model-informed drug development asks developers to rate the model influence and the consequence of a wrong decision, derives the model risk from them, and says that model evaluation should be commensurate with that risk ([1](#ref-1)). I applied these assessment elements to three simulated dose-selection analyses in which the truth is known: a pooled exposure–response model that selects one of two doses when severity drives clearance, a tumour growth inhibition model fitted with exponential growth to Gompertz tumours, and a population pharmacokinetic (PK) model fitted by the R package nlme to sparse samples. For each case I asked which checks would have flagged the failure. Where severity drove clearance, the pooled model chose the correct dose in only 25.4% of 2,000 near-plateau trials at 40 patients per arm; the exposure slope was significant in 55.2% of 1,000 trials, whereas the comparison of the model-based gain with the randomized arm contrast flagged 10.5%. For tumour growth, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) favoured the Gompertz model in at least 96% of studies in 8 of 9 settings but in 46.0% in the nearly exponential case with a 14-day study. For the sparse population PK model with 40 subjects, the typical absorption rate constant was underestimated by 29.4% with 2 samples per subject, and fits without a convergence warning had the same bias. In these simulations the ratings did not determine how sensitive a check was: the checks that compared the model with independent or richer information flagged the failures that I had built in, in 10.5% to 100% of studies, whereas convergence, in-sample significance and in-window fit did not. These sensitivities are conditional on the single failure mode and effect size set in each case, and the ratings are my own and were assigned after the results were known; they are not a regulatory assessment.

## Introduction {#article-introduction}

The ICH M15 guideline was adopted at Step 4 on 29 January 2026 and was announced by the US Food and Drug Administration as final guidance on 3 June 2026 ([1](#ref-1), [2](#ref-2)). It defines a framework for assessing evidence from model-informed drug development (MIDD) in which the question of interest, the context of use, the model influence and the consequence of a wrong decision are described, the last two are rated low, medium or high with a justification, and the model risk is derived from them ([1](#ref-1), Section 2.1). The guideline states that model evaluation should meet current accepted standards and be commensurate with the model risk ([1](#ref-1), Sections 2.1.5 and 3). What a given rating implies for the evaluation is left to the analyst and the reviewers.

This article takes the ratings as the starting point and asks what checks that such an evaluation could include would find. **For three dose-selection analyses with known truth, what do the M15 assessment elements say about the model risk, and which evaluation steps flag the failure that the simulation reveals?** The simulations were run for other questions and are described in separate articles ([3](#ref-3)–[5](#ref-5)). Here they are reused as cases in which the truth is available, so that a check can be scored by how often it flags the problem.

## Scope and sources {#article-scope}

I read the final text of M15 ([1](#ref-1)), in particular Section 2.1 (key assessment elements), Section 2.2 (technical criteria and appropriateness), Section 3 (model evaluation) and Section 4.1 (model analysis plan), and took the bibliographic data of the US notice from the Federal Register ([2](#ref-2)). I did not read the supplementary training materials of ICH, which the guideline says are in development ([1](#ref-1), footnote 5), and I do not know how a regulator would rate these cases. The ratings in Table 1 are mine, for simulated settings with assumed consequences, and I assigned them after the simulations had been run and their results were known, so they are post hoc. Model impact (Section 2.1.6) is not assessed here because it concerns the effect of the model on the development program, which does not arise for simulated analyses.

The three cases are my own simulation studies ([3](#ref-3)–[5](#ref-5)). Their numbers come from the results files of those studies, listed under data and code availability. Two checks that those studies did not record were computed for this article: for case 1 the in-sample and arm-contrast checks of the exposure–response model (1,000 trials per cell, seed 20261124), and for case 3 a likelihood comparison of the full and the reduced random-effects model (200 studies per cell, seed 20261125). Each simulated study is a patient population or an animal study with known parameters, so a check is scored by the share of studies in which it flags the problem.

## Ratings for four questions of interest {#article-ratings}

M15 recommends a separate assessment table for each question of interest ([1](#ref-1), Section 2.1.1). Case 2 has two questions, a potency ranking and a projection beyond the study window, so there are four rows (Table 1). The model influence is rated by the weight of the model outcomes relative to the other evidence ([1](#ref-1), Section 2.1.3); when the model is the sole source for the decision the influence is generally high. The consequence of a wrong decision considers the severity and the likelihood of the negative effect ([1](#ref-1), Section 2.1.4). Model risk is derived from the two and may follow the more influential of them when they differ ([1](#ref-1), Section 2.1.5).

## Case 1: a pooled exposure–response model and the randomized contrast {#article-case1}

In the near-plateau setting the true response gain of the high dose is about 0.03, below the boundary of 0.08, so the correct dose is the low one. Without confounding the pooled exposure–response model picked it in 73.8% of 2,000 trials at 40 patients per arm and 83.8% at 100, against 71.5% and 74.7% for the rule that uses the arm response rates. When severity drove clearance (49% higher clearance per SD of severity, assumed), the model picked the correct dose in 25.4% and 11.9% of trials, against 73.0% and 77.0% for the arm rule ([3](#ref-3)). The fitted exposure slope averaged 0.87 against a true 0.2, and adding the true severity brought it to 0.23 ([3](#ref-3)).

Which checks saw this? The in-sample checks supported the model. The likelihood-ratio test of the exposure slope was significant in 55.2% of trials at 40 per arm and 89.9% at 100 under confounding, against 6.9% and 10.8% without it (Table 2): the data appeared to support an exposure–response relationship that, as far as the dose decision goes, was largely severity. The agreement of fitted and observed response rates by arm was within 0.02 at every arm in 23.3% (40 per arm) and 13.5% (100 per arm) of confounded trials and in 42.4% and 61.9% of unconfounded trials. At 100 per arm the check therefore separated the settings, but with a high false-alarm rate (38.1% of unconfounded trials exceeded 0.02, against 86.5% of confounded trials), and the threshold of 0.02 is arbitrary. The slope test and the arm-contrast comparison answer different questions: the first asks whether exposure predicts response, the second whether the model-based gain agrees with the randomized contrast.

The check that did discriminate compares the model-based gain with the randomized arm contrast, which is the information the model does not use for its slope. The gap exceeded 1.96 standard errors of the arm contrast in 10.5% (40 per arm) and 26.5% (100 per arm) of confounded trials and in 0.2% and 0.5% without confounding. It almost never raises a false alarm and it detects the problem in a minority of trials; because the standard error of the arm contrast ignores that the model-based gain and the contrast come from the same data, the rule is conservative and its sensitivity is a lower bound. The model and the arm rule disagreed on whether the response gain reached 0.08 (the toxicity cap of the dose-selection study was not applied here) in 49.1% and 65.1% of confounded trials, so the disagreement was far more common than the formal flag, but the same disagreement occurred in 17.8% and 16.8% of unconfounded trials, so it does not identify which of the two is wrong.

M15 asks that key assumptions be identified and justified and that alternatives be considered and that robustness be assessed, and it notes that external validation with independent data may be essential in some cases ([1](#ref-1), Section 3). Here, the alternative that matters is a model that includes a measure of severity: the version with a noisy severity marker lifted the correct-dose share from 25.4% to 52.2% at 40 per arm, still below the arm rule ([3](#ref-3)). With a high model influence and a high consequence of a wrong decision (Table 1), a check against independent information is what I would call for. The randomized arm contrast is information from the same trial and not an external validation, and it was the most sensitive check available within the trial, though a low-powered one at these sample sizes.

## Case 2: a growth model whose misfit grows with the projection horizon {#article-case2}

For vehicle tumours that grow in a Gompertz way, the exponential-growth model recovered the half-maximal concentration (median 2.99 to 3.01 in all settings) and underestimated the maximal kill rate (0.153, 0.135 and 0.121 per day after 14, 21 and 28 days at a Gompertz rate of 0.05, against 0.20) ([4](#ref-4)). The projected treated-to-vehicle volume ratio for a concentration inside the tested range was 0.459, 0.619 and 0.784 of the true ratio at day 28 for those durations, and at the last observation day 0.930, 0.863 and 0.784 ([4](#ref-4)).

The question of interest decides which of these matters. For the potency ranking (row 2a of Table 1) the exponential model's recovery of the half-maximal concentration is what counts, and the low model risk is consistent with the result. For the projection (row 2b) the evaluation has to cover the horizon: the exponential model's in-window error at the last observation day after 14 days was moderate (0.930 of the true ratio), but its projection to day 28 was off by more than half (0.459) (Table 3). A fit within the observed window does not cover this, because it is the applicability of the model to a horizon that the data do not reach. M15 distinguishes validation, the overall comparison of the model with the data, from applicability assessment, which concerns the adequacy of the data and the model for each intended use ([1](#ref-1), Section 3).

The comparison with a richer model was sensitive in most settings. AIC favoured the Gompertz model in 98.8% of studies after 14 days and in 100% after 21 and 28 days at a Gompertz rate of 0.05, and in all three durations at a rate of 0.10 ([4](#ref-4)). In the nearly exponential case (rate 0.02) it did so in 46.0% of studies after 14 days and 96.0% after 21 days (Table 3). The median residual sum of squares was only 6.4% lower for the Gompertz model after 14 days at a rate of 0.05 (5.43 against 5.80), and 33.1% lower after 28 days (9.23 against 13.79) ([4](#ref-4)), so an inspection of the fit in the window would not by itself have indicated the size of the projection error. The 14-day study at a rate of 0.02 is the case in which the check lets the problem through while the day-28 projection is still 0.641 of the true ratio.

## Case 3: a likelihood comparison that does not see the omitted random effect where it matters {#article-case3}

For a one-compartment oral model fitted by nlme to 40 subjects, the typical absorption rate constant was underestimated by 29.4% with 2 samples per subject and by 6.9% with 6, and the share of studies in which the SD of the random effect on V was estimated at zero was 41.0% and 1.8% ([5](#ref-5)). Two estimation flags were available. nlme returned a fit in 97.5% of studies with 2 samples, but a fit without a convergence warning in only 46.3%. Restricting the analysis to fits without a warning left the bias in place (typical ka −29.3% with 2 samples, −7.3% with 6) (Table 4), so the convergence flag identifies unreliable fits without identifying the fits that are biased. M15 defines verification as ensuring that the code, equations and calculations are correct ([1](#ref-1), Section 3); a correct implementation can still produce a biased estimator in a small sample, which verification does not address. The convergence flag and the significance of the exposure slope are diagnostics of estimation and fit, not verification in this sense.

The structural misspecification examined in that study was omitting the random effect on ka, which the data-generating model contained. In that reduced model the typical values were no worse, but the residual SD was overestimated by 23.5% to 31.0% and the SD of the random effect on V by 18.8% to 29.0% at 40 subjects ([5](#ref-5)), so a reader of the typical values alone would not see it. A likelihood comparison of the full and the reduced model (AIC) preferred the full model, which has the data-generating structure, in 30.6% of 200 studies with 2 samples per subject, 82.0% with 3, 99.5% with 4 and 100% with 6 (Table 4). With 2 samples the comparison preferred the wrong model more often than the right one, and only 26.5% of the studies had both fits free of convergence warnings (30.2% of those 53 studies preferred the full model). With 2 samples per subject, the 3 random effects exceed the observations per subject, the standard deviations of the full model often collapsed to the boundary, and the median AIC difference stayed at −2.0, so the comparison is not well posed there. The check that detects the omission therefore needed 3 to 4 samples per subject, while the bias in the typical values was already large at 3 samples (typical ka −20.1%).

## What the ratings fixed and what they left open {#article-discussion}

In all three cases the rating bore on the depth of evaluation that M15 expects and not on the sensitivity of a check. M15 does not state which checks correspond to a given rating; in my judgment, the evaluation for the high-risk cases should have included checks that compare the model with independent or richer information: the randomized arm contrast in case 1, the richer growth model in case 2, the full random-effects model in case 3. Their sensitivity ran from 10.5% to 100% across the settings in Figure 1, and it depended on the design variable that decides the information content of the data: patients per arm, study duration, samples per subject. The in-sample checks (convergence, exposure slope significance, fit within the window) are diagnostics of estimation and fit and not verification in the sense of M15, that is, correct code and calculations, and they did not discriminate. They were most reassuring where the failure was largest in absolute terms: the exposure slope was significant in 89.9% of the confounded trials at 100 per arm, where the model chose the correct dose in 11.9%. These sensitivities are conditional on the single failure mode that I created in each case and on the effect size I chose.

Two features of M15 bear directly on these findings. First, the technical criteria and the model evaluation are to be predefined in a model analysis plan, with "pre-define" meaning documented before the data are accessed or the analysis is performed ([1](#ref-1), Section 4.1 and footnote 10); a criterion such as "the model-based gain must agree with the arm contrast within 1.96 standard errors" could only be set in advance, and at 40 per arm it would have been met in 89.5% of the confounded trials, in which the model chose the correct dose in 25.4%. Second, the guideline treats the model risk as a property of the model outcomes for a specific question and not as intrinsic to the method ([1](#ref-1), Section 2.1.5), which is what the two rows for the tumour growth model show: the same fit has a low risk for ranking potency and a higher risk for projection, and the evaluation that suits the first would not reveal the second.

The cases also show where the ratings could mislead. The medium rating for the population PK model (Table 1) rests on the assumption that later data or a dose adjustment can correct an exposure miss, and I did not test whether a later dose adjustment would correct a 20% to 29% error in the typical ka, which is what 2 to 3 samples per subject gave. The consequence rating for case 1 was judged from assumed rates of response and toxicity, and a different severity of the toxic effect would change it.

### Limitations

The ratings, and the sensitivity of the checks, apply to the simulated settings and to my judgments of influence and consequence; a regulator or a multidisciplinary team could rate them differently, and M15 does not prescribe a rule that maps two ratings to a third. The cases are simulations with a known truth, so the shares are the sensitivity of a check to a misspecification that I created; real misspecification is not known, and no regulatory decision was involved. The checks compared are a selection: other diagnostics (visual predictive checks, residual plots, standard errors, shrinkage) were not computed. Case 1 used 1,000 trials per cell for the checks and 2,000 for the dose-selection results; the shares come from different random draws (seed 20261124 for the checks), so the two sets of columns in Table 2 are not paired. In case 3 the likelihood comparison used 200 studies per cell, with a Monte Carlo standard error of about 0.035. The in-sample check of agreement of fitted and observed arm rates used a threshold of 0.02, and the 1.96 standard error rule is a descriptive rule chosen here, not a regulatory criterion, and it is conservative. The criteria and the evaluation in M15 are agreed with the assessor in advance for a real context of use, which does not exist for these simulated cases, so applying the assessment elements to them illustrates the framework and does not test it. I read only the guideline and the notice; the supplementary training materials and any regulator's practice were not available to me.

## Conclusion {#article-conclusion}

Applying the M15 assessment elements to three simulated dose-selection analyses gave a high model risk for the exposure–response model, a medium risk for the population PK model, and a question-dependent risk for the tumour growth model (low for potency, high for projection). The checks that flagged the failures were those that compared the model with independent or richer information, and their sensitivity ranged from 10.5% to 100% of studies depending on the patients per arm, the study duration and the samples per subject; the convergence, in-sample significance and in-window fit checks did not flag the failures that cost the most. The conclusions hold for the failure modes and effect sizes that I set, and my own post hoc ratings. The conclusions hold for the three assumed settings and my own ratings.
